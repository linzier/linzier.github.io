PHP Development Reflections: Thoughts on Backend Development

2020-04-20 | Software Engineering

Introduction

After years of working with PHP and diverse backend technologies, I have accumulated a set of observations about what goes wrong in large-scale systems and how we can construct them more thoughtfully. This article is a collection of those reflections — the recurring pitfalls I have observed, the architectural patterns that offer relief, and the conceptual clarifications I wish someone had shared with me earlier in my career.

Current Problems in Backend Development

Business Logic Leaking Into the Wrong Layers

One of the most pervasive issues in backend codebases is business logic creeping into layers where it does not belong. Controllers become bloated with conditional rules that belong in domain services. Views perform calculations that should be handled by models. Database queries embed business policies that should be encapsulated in repositories. When logic leaks across boundaries, the system becomes a tangled web where modifying a single business requirement demands changes to multiple unrelated files.

God Services

A "God Service" is a class or module that knows too much and does too much. It handles user authentication, orchestrates payments, sends emails, and generates reports — all within the same 3,000-line file. God services emerge organically as developers add "just one more quick feature" to an existing class. They are notoriously difficult to test, understand, and modify without causing cascading regressions.

Duplicate Functionality

In mature, multi-developer codebases, you will often find duplicate implementations of the exact same business logic. One module calculates shipping fees one way, while another uses a slightly different formula. Neither is technically "incorrect," but the lack of consistency inevitably leads to bugs and administrative overhead. This duplication is a symptom of poor modularity and siloed team communication.

Anemic Models

An anemic model is a domain object that acts as a passive bag of data (with only getters and setters) but lacks any real behavior. All business logic is extracted into external service classes that manipulate the model's state. This directly contradicts the core premise of object-oriented design, where objects are meant to encapsulate both data and the behavior operating on that data.

Tech-Oriented Module Division

Many systems are structured by technical layer rather than by business capability. Developers organize code into folders like controllers/, services/, repositories/, and models/. Consequently, tracing a single business feature turns into a jarring exercise in folder-hopping. While this organization is convenient from a purely technical standpoint, it obscures the actual business domain.

Frontend-Backend Decoupling and Logic Duplication

When the frontend and backend are developed as isolated projects, teams are often tempted to duplicate validation, transformations, and business rules in both codebases. The frontend validates user input using its own logic, while the backend repeats the process on the server. When a business rule changes, both codebases must be carefully updated in tandem. This duplication introduces high maintenance costs and is a breeding ground for subtle, frustrating inconsistencies.

Framework Impedance Mismatch and Conway's Law

Conway's Law states that the architecture of a system tends to mirror the communication patterns of the organization that built it. When you adopt an opinionated monolithic framework, your application inevitably conforms to its architecture. Likewise, organizing teams into strict frontend and backend silos guarantees a system split along those exact lines. The framework you choose shapes how you perceive and solve problems — and those default conventions are not always aligned with your domain's needs.

OOP Skepticism

After observing years of poorly applied object-oriented design — massive classes, deep and fragile inheritance trees, and gratuitous abstractions — many developers become cynical about OOP as a whole. They declare object-oriented programming to be overhyped and pivot completely to functional or procedural paradigms. However, the root problem is not OOP itself, but rather the superficial application of its ideas without a deep understanding of cohesion, coupling, and abstraction.

Design Patterns That Help

IoC / DI: Dependency Inversion

The Dependency Inversion Principle (the "D" in SOLID) states that high-level modules should not depend on low-level modules; both must depend on abstractions. In practice, this means a service should not be responsible for instantiating its own dependencies; instead, those dependencies should be injected from the outside.

Consider a service that dispatches email notifications. In a naive implementation, the service instantiates a concrete EmailSender directly. This tightly couples the service to a specific delivery mechanism. By adopting dependency injection, the service instead accepts an EmailSenderInterface in its constructor. The actual implementation (SMTP, SendGrid, or a mock for unit testing) is determined and injected externally.

The fundamental insight here is the separation of usage from construction. The service consumes the dependency but remains completely agnostic about how it is created. This decoupling makes the service easy to test with test doubles, straightforward to configure, and highly readable due to its explicit contract.

Hexagonal Architecture (Ports and Adapters)

Hexagonal Architecture, commonly known as Ports and Adapters, is an architectural pattern that shields the core business logic from infrastructural concerns. The heart of the system (the hexagon) contains the pure domain model and business rules. It interacts with the outside world strictly through defined ports (interfaces) and adapters (implementations).

Adapters are divided into two main categories:

  • Driving adapters (left side) — triggers that invoke the application, such as HTTP controllers, CLI commands, or message queue consumers.
  • Driven adapters (right side) — infrastructure invoked by the application, such as databases, external APIs, or file storage systems.

The core business logic never depends on external adapters; it depends solely on ports (interfaces). The adapters themselves implement or consume these ports. This inversion of control guarantees that your business logic remains isolated and highly testable. You can swap out your database engine, web framework, or message broker without modifying a single line of business logic.

GRASP: Information Expert and Creator Patterns

GRASP (General Responsibility Assignment Software Patterns) offers invaluable guidelines for assigning responsibilities to classes:

  • Information Expert — assign a responsibility to the class that possesses the information required to fulfill it. For instance, if you need to calculate an order's total price, the Order class is the expert because it holds the collection of line items.
  • Creator — assign the responsibility of instantiating an object to the class that has the necessary initialization data, or closely uses/contains the created object. This provides a structured alternative to routing all object creation through factories.

Leveraging GRASP helps you make clean, localized decisions about where behaviors belong, preventing the creation of bloated God services.

SOLID Principles

Single Responsibility Principle (SRP)

A class should have one, and only one, reason to change. This does not imply that a class must only contain a single method; rather, it means a class should be answerable to exactly one actor or stakeholder. A class that handles both business calculations and database persistence has two distinct reasons to change: when business rules shift, and when the database schema is refactored.

Open/Closed Principle (OCP)

Software artifacts should be open for extension but closed for modification. You should be able to introduce new features without altering existing, working code. This is achieved via abstractions: new behavior is introduced by writing new implementations of an interface, rather than patching existing classes.

Liskov Substitution Principle (LSP)

Subtypes must be substitutable for their base types. If class B extends class A, you should be able to replace instances of A with B without altering the correctness of the program. Subclasses must honor the contract of their parent, neither strengthening preconditions nor weakening postconditions. A classic violation is a Square class extending a Rectangle: forcing the width and height to be equal violates the rectangular expectation that width and height can vary independently.

Interface Segregation Principle (ISP)

Clients should not be forced to depend on interfaces they do not use. Fat interfaces with unrelated methods should be split into smaller, cohesive, role-specific interfaces. A class that only requires a single method should not carry the burden of implementing dozens of unused ones.

Dependency Inversion Principle (DIP)

As discussed above under IoC/DI, high-level policy-making modules must not depend on low-level utility modules. Both must depend on abstractions.

Other Important Patterns

DDD: Domain-Driven Design

Domain-Driven Design (DDD) is an approach to software design that centers on the business domain. Its key pillars are:

  • Ubiquitous Language — the same terminology should be used consistently from business requirements documents to variable and class names in the code. If the business team refers to a transaction as a "booking," the codebase must have a Booking class, not a ResEntity or TblReservation.
  • Code as the Model — the code is not just an implementation of the model; it is the model in executable form. If the code compiles but violates real-world business constraints, the model is incorrect.
  • Bounded Contexts — large, complex systems are partitioned into discrete bounded contexts, each maintaining its own independent model and vocabulary. The concept of an "Order" in a sales context may represent something completely different from an "Order" in a shipping context.

CQRS: Command Query Responsibility Segregation

CQRS splits the read model from the write model. Under this pattern, commands (writes) and queries (reads) are handled by completely different classes, and can even utilize distinct, optimized data stores.

  • Command side — responsible for input validation, business rule enforcement, and state persistence. It is highly optimized for transaction consistency and write correctness.
  • Query side — responsible for fetching and preparing data for presentation. It is optimized for speed and can leverage denormalized views, aggressive caching, or replica read databases.

CQRS is particularly useful when read and write workloads exhibit wildly different scaling characteristics (e.g., millions of reads to thousands of writes), or when the write domain contains rich, intricate business logic while reads require simple, flat representations.

Concept Analysis and Clarifications

Service: A Coordinator, Not an Implementer

An application service should be a thin coordinator that orchestrates domain objects rather than performing actual business logic. It loads the entity, invokes a domain method, persists the updated state, and dispatches side-effect notifications. A common architectural failure is designing systems around procedurally-driven services operating on dumb, passive data structures. Instead, focus on building rich, self-contained domain objects, and let services act merely as their orchestrators.

Do not ask "what service handles this?" Ask "which domain object is the natural owner of this behavior?"

Database-Oriented Programming: The Root of Architectural Decay

Too many systems are designed from the database schema upward rather than the business domain outward. Database tables dictate class structures, columns dictate properties, and foreign keys dictate relationships. This is database-oriented programming, and it leads directly to anemic models, business logic locked away in database triggers or stored procedures, and a codebase that mirrors SQL storage rather than business intent.

The database is an implementation detail. It is a persistence mechanism, not the source of truth for your business model. Design your rich domain model first, then determine how to serialize and persist it.

Repository: A Collection-Like Interface, Not a DAO

A repository should behave like an in-memory collection of domain objects. When you query it for an order by its ID, it should return a fully formed Order domain entity, not a raw database row or an active record instance. A DAO (Data Access Object), by contrast, is a thin wrapper over SQL queries designed to return flat Data Transfer Objects (DTOs).

The Repository pattern abstracts persistence entirely. The domain layer should remain oblivious to whether the entity was fetched from a relational database, a NoSQL document store, or an in-memory test double.

Database transactions belong in the application service layer, never in the repository. A repository method should not be responsible for beginning and committing transactions. The application service, which coordinates multiple operations across different repositories, must define the transaction boundary. This ensures that the entire business workflow is atomic.

Controller: An HTTP-to-Domain Adapter

A controller is a delivery-mechanism adapter whose sole job is to translate user inputs (such as HTTP requests or CLI arguments) into domain invocations. It is never the place for business calculations. However, it is the ideal place to enforce request-level access control. Determining whether an incoming request has the authority to trigger an action is a boundary concern that belongs at the edge of the system, keeping the core domain clean and focused.

Entity: Defined by Identity and Lifecycle

An entity is an object defined primarily by its persistent identity rather than its transient attributes. Two orders with identical totals but different IDs are distinct entities. Entities possess a lifecycle — they are created, mutated, and eventually archived or deleted. This contrasts with value objects, which are defined entirely by their attributes and are strictly immutable. A Money object holding 100 and USD is identical to any other Money object holding those same values; it has no identity and can be safely discarded. An Order with ID 123 is unique; it is an entity.

Modeling: An Evolutionary, Iterative Journey

Domain modeling is not a waterfall phase where you design a UML diagram once and translate it directly into code. It is an evolutionary process that matures as your understanding of the business domain deepens. Start with raw requirements, identify terms and constraints, and refine the model through continuous implementation and testing. Let the business language shape your classes, not static diagrams.

Frameworks, SDKs, and Vendors: Taming Conway's Law

The frameworks you choose heavily influence your system's architecture. Monolithic frameworks (like Laravel or Spring) steer developers toward monolithic structures. Conversely, a composition of small, single-purpose libraries encourages deep modularity.

Whenever practical, prefer composing focused packages over relying blindly on monolithic frameworks. Composing libraries gives you complete ownership of your architecture and minimizes the friction between your business rules and framework constraints.

That said, frameworks provide undeniable utility by eliminating boilerplate, standardizing common tasks, and reducing decision fatigue. The critical discipline is to treat the framework as an external tool — do not let it dictate your domain. When framework conventions clash with your core domain model, the domain model must always take precedence.

Conclusion

Backend engineering is difficult not because technical stacks are complex, but because business domains are inherently messy. Our greatest challenge is not choosing a database or picking a framework; it is understanding the business, translating it into an elegant model, and structuring the codebase so it can gracefully evolve as the business scales.

Architectural principles and patterns — DDD, hexagonal architecture, SOLID, CQRS — are not silver bullets. They are tools designed to manage cognitive load and maintain modularity. Apply them pragmatically where they offer clarity, and discard them where they introduce unnecessary overhead. The goal is never to build a "perfectly pattern-compliant" codebase; the goal is to build software that is understandable, maintainable, and highly responsive to change.
