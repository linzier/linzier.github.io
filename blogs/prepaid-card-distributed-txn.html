Distributed Transaction Design: Lessons from a Prepaid Card Recharge System

2020-04-20 | Distributed Systems

The Original Implementation

The prepaid card recharge system initially launched with a deceptively simple design. When a user initiated a recharge, the system executed three sequential actions:

  1. Deduct the specified amount from the user's payment account.
  2. Credit the matching amount to the prepaid card balance.
  3. Record the transaction history.

In this naive architecture, all operations were bundled inside a single service method and executed sequentially. There was no distributed transaction handling to speak of. While this approach performed adequately during periods of low traffic and stable networks, it was an architectural ticking time bomb — and it didn't take long to explode.

The Architectural Pitfalls

Complete Absence of Failure Recovery

The original design relied on a perfect, uninterrupted "happy path." If the payment deduction succeeded but the downstream card recharge failed due to a network blip, the user was left in financial limbo — their funds were deducted, but never credited. The system offered no automated rollbacks, no compensation tasks, and no out-of-band reconciliation. For any platform handling financial value, this is an unacceptable vulnerability.

Business Logic Creep in the Delivery Layer

A significant portion of the transactional business logic was allowed to creep into frontend controllers and the outer orchestration layer, rather than being encapsulated within a dedicated domain service. The controllers were directly managing payment validations, manual balance arithmetic, and raw exception handling. This serious leak violated the separation of concerns, making unit testing near impossible and complicating modifications.

Lack of Boundary Pre-checks

The system did not actively validate the preconditions of a recharge before initiating state modifications. It bypassed crucial safety checks: whether the target card was active and unblocked, whether the incoming amount would breach the card's maximum balance ceiling, and whether the customer possessed sufficient funds. Instead, these constraints were evaluated implicitly during database writes, leading to cryptic errors and messy partial failures.

The Fallacy of Infallible Downstream Services

The legacy code treated the downstream card recharge service as a deterministic, infallible system. In reality, any remote network service is subject to diverse failure modes: transient timeouts, database lock contention, unexpected container restarts, and physical infrastructure disruptions. Assuming 100% downstream reliability is a guaranteed recipe for persistent data corruption.

The Refactoring Journey

1. Extracting a Dedicated Recharge Service

To establish clean boundaries, we extracted all recharge mechanics into a highly cohesive, dedicated RechargeService. This service became the exclusive custodian of recharge business policies: enforcing structural validation, checking balance ceilings, calculating state transitions, and logging immutable transaction intents. The controller was reduced to a thin adapter responsible solely for parsing requests and formatting HTTP responses.

2. Implementing Robust Pre-checks

Before any currency or state is mutated, the RechargeService triggers a comprehensive, read-only pre-flight check:

  • Is the prepaid card active and not flagged for fraud or suspension?
  • Will the requested amount push the card's current balance beyond its legal maximum ceiling?
  • Does the user's source payment account hold sufficient funds?
  • Are there any unresolved concurrent transactions active on this specific card?

If any pre-flight assertion fails, the entire transaction is rejected immediately. Because this happens before any write operations occur, we completely avoid partial failures and keep error states clean.

3. Designing for Graceful Failures

If the pre-flight checks pass but the card service returns an explicit rejection during the recharge attempt, the system must immediately initiate a compensation task (a refund). This brings us to the core problem of distributed transactions: How do you guarantee that the payment deduction and the card recharge either both succeed or both roll back, when they involve independent, physically separated services?

The Timeout Dilemma

The naive approach to handling downstream rejections is: "If the recharge call fails, trigger a refund." However, this synchronous rollback model collapses when the failure is a network timeout.

Consider this classic distributed systems scenario:

  1. The orchestration service successfully deducts the payment.
  2. The system issues a synchronous call to the downstream card recharge service.
  3. The card service receives the request, processes it successfully, and updates the card balance.
  4. However, the network drops the return packet. The caller waits, hits its timeout threshold, and throws a network exception.

From the orchestrator's perspective, the operation timed out. If it interprets this timeout as a definitive failure and immediately triggers an automated refund, a severe inconsistency occurs: The customer has been refunded their money, yet their prepaid card was successfully credited. The platform has double-spent, resulting in a direct financial loss.

In a distributed system, you can never interpret a network timeout as a failure. A timeout represents absolute uncertainty — the request is in an indeterminate third state: It may have succeeded, it may have failed, or it may still be processing.

Why "Query-Before-Refund" is a Fallacy

A common, superficial fix is to check state before acting: "Before initiating a refund, query the card service to check if the balance was credited. If it was, mark it as a success; if not, proceed with the refund."

While this sounds logical, it fails under real-world production conditions:

  • The query itself can time out: If the network or the downstream database is suffering from high latency, your status query is just as likely to time out as the original mutation request, keeping you in an uncertain state.
  • The mutation may still be in flight: The downstream card service might have received the request and queued it for execution. If you query it at that exact millisecond, it will return "not found." You then proceed with a refund, only for the queued mutation to complete successfully moments later, triggering the double-credit bug.
  • Race conditions: Without strict distributed locking, there is an unavoidable race condition between checking the state and executing the refund. You cannot atomically query and mutate across separate physical network barriers.

The Robust Solution: Scheduled Out-of-Band Reconciliation

To build an unbreakable system, we must design for eventual consistency and accept transient uncertainty. Instead of trying to force a synchronous resolution, the system decouples state mutation from state reconciliation:

  1. The system records the recharge attempt in a local database with a status of "pending."
  2. If the card service returns an immediate, definitive success response, the transaction is marked as "completed."
  3. If the card service returns an immediate, definitive rejection (not a timeout), the transaction is marked as "failed" and an automated refund is queued.
  4. If the card service call times out, the system **does nothing synchronously**. It leaves the transaction status as "pending" and returns an "in-progress" notification to the user.
  5. A scheduled reconciliation task (cron or distributed worker) runs periodically (e.g., every 10 minutes) and queries transactions that have remained in a "pending" state for longer than a specified safety threshold (e.g., 30 minutes).

This time window is critical. It provides an adequate buffer for any in-flight downstream operations to finalize or fail. By the time the reconciliation task executes, the state of the downstream system has settled, allowing the worker to make a deterministic decision: complete the transaction or issue a refund.

Adding a Sentinel: The "Recharging" Transaction Log

To protect against application server crashes, we introduced a write-ahead state sentinel. The complete transaction lifecycle now unfolds as follows:

  1. Before initiating any external network calls, create an immutable transaction record in the local database with the status RECHARGING.
  2. Deduct the funds from the user's payment account.
  3. Call the downstream card recharge service.
  4. If the call succeeds, update the local record status to SUCCESS.
  5. If the call fails with a definitive error, update the local record status to REFUNDED and execute the compensation workflow.
  6. If a timeout occurs, leave the status as RECHARGING and exit the request loop.

This RECHARGING record acts as an architectural sentinel. Even if the entire application cluster crashes or restarts midway through an operation, the state is persisted. When the cluster recovers, the scheduled reconciliation task scans for stale RECHARGING records and safely resolves them.

Key Architectural Takeaways

1. Remote Invocations Lack Ordering and Timeliness Guarantees

In any distributed system, you must abandon assumptions of physical ordering or instant delivery. Network packets can be delayed, duplicated, or lost. Design your systems from the ground up to be idempotent and tolerant of out-of-order execution.

2. Proactively Minimize the Surface Area for Exceptions

While you cannot fully eliminate network failures, you can aggressively reduce their probability. Utilizing thorough pre-checks, strict idempotency keys, and circuit breakers significantly limits the frequency of unresolved states, keeping your system running smoothly.

3. Implement Dedicated Reconciliation Sentinels

Every distributed transaction boundary requires an out-of-band reconciliation engine. This background sentinel acts as your safety net, catching the complex, rare edge cases that synchronous request-response loops simply cannot resolve safely.

In distributed architectures, consistency is not achieved by attempting to prevent failures; it is achieved by accepting that failures are inevitable, and designing background processes to automatically detect and reconcile inconsistencies.

Summary

Refactoring our prepaid card recharge system proved that distributed transaction consistency cannot be solved via simple synchronous request-response patterns. Timeouts are not failures — they are states of absolute uncertainty. Synchronous "query-before-refund" flows introduce severe race conditions. The only resilient path is to embrace eventual consistency: log your intent, attempt the mutation, and resolve the outcome asynchronously.

The three pillars of robust distributed transaction design are:

  1. Defensive Pre-checking: Validate all business invariants before modifying any state.
  2. Failure Minimization: Leverage idempotency, timeouts, and circuit breakers to reduce the likelihood of inconsistent states.
  3. Asynchronous Reconciliation: Deploy dedicated out-of-band sentinels to proactively repair state mismatches.
