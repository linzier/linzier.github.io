Technical Interviews (Part 1): The Interviewer's Perspective

2020-04-20 | Career

Most articles about technical interviews focus on the candidate's perspective: how to prepare, how to perform, how to land the offer. But today I want to flip the camera around and look at the process from the other side of the table — the interviewer's perspective. Understanding what goes through an interviewer's mind can make you a far more effective candidate.

Resume Screening: The One-Minute Test

Here is a blunt truth: I spend roughly one minute on each resume. Sometimes less, sometimes a bit more, but rarely much longer. When you are dealing with dozens or even hundreds of applications, you have no choice but to triage. The goal is not to find the perfect candidate — it is to identify signals that suggest someone is above average.

Self-Introduction: Not a Laundry List

When a candidate's resume includes a self-introduction section, I look for one thing above all: does this person show genuine passion and confidence, or are they just reciting a list of technologies?

A bad self-introduction reads like a grocery list: "I know Java, Python, MySQL, Redis, Kafka, Docker..." That tells me nothing. Anyone can learn a technology; listing it proves nothing.

A good self-introduction reveals character. It shows what excites the person, what they have built, what problems they care about. Confidence without arrogance, enthusiasm without hand-waving — that is what catches my eye.

Project Experience: Contributions, Not Descriptions

Most resumes describe projects the way a product manager would: "This system handles 10,000 QPS and supports features A, B, and C." That is a project description, not a personal contribution.

What I want to see is: what did YOU do? What design decisions did you make? What problems did you solve? What trade-offs did you navigate? "I redesigned the caching layer, reducing average response time by 40%" tells me infinitely more than "Responsible for system optimization."

Blog and Open Source: The Huge Bonus

A candidate who maintains a technical blog or contributes to open source projects immediately stands out. Why? Because these activities demonstrate several qualities at once:

  • Passion — they code even when nobody is paying them to.
  • Communication skills — they can explain technical concepts in writing.
  • Consistency — maintaining a blog or contributing regularly requires discipline.
  • Depth — writing about a topic forces you to understand it deeply.

If I see a blog link on a resume, I will click it. If the content is thoughtful and well-written, that candidate jumps to the top of my pile.

The Written Test: Fundamentals Over Trivia

A good written test should not be a quiz show. I have no interest in asking candidates to memorize obscure trivia or puzzle through brain teasers. What I want to test is whether they have solid fundamentals.

The written test should cover three areas:

  1. Language fundamentals — do they truly understand the tools they use every day?
  2. Algorithms and data structures — can they think algorithmically?
  3. Design patterns and system thinking — can they design clean, maintainable code?

One policy I strongly believe in: candidates are allowed to Google during the written test. In real work, you Google things constantly. Testing whether someone can find and apply information quickly is far more practical than testing whether they have memorized an API.

The Face-to-Face Interview

First Impressions Matter

Whether we like it or not, first impressions shape the entire interview. Punctuality, appearance, body language — these are not superficial details. They signal respect for the process and awareness of social norms. A candidate who arrives ten minutes late, disheveled, and without eye contact has already started in a hole.

Self-Introduction: Show Value and Ownership

The face-to-face self-introduction serves a different purpose than the one on the resume. Here, I am looking for a narrative. Can the candidate tell a coherent story about their career? Can they highlight what makes them unique?

The best introductions I have heard follow a simple pattern: "I am a [role] with [X] years of experience. My focus has been on [domain]. At [company], I led/designed/built [thing], which achieved [result]. I am particularly passionate about [area], and that is what brought me here today."

Notice the pattern: identity, expertise, accomplishment, passion, motivation. Every sentence adds value.

Project Q&A: Design Decisions and Progressive Deepening

This is the heart of the technical interview. I pick one or two projects from the resume and go deep. But I am not trying to trip the candidate up — I am trying to understand the depth of their involvement and their thinking process.

I start broad: "Tell me about the architecture of this system." Then I narrow: "Why did you choose this database over that one?" Then deeper: "What happened when the data volume grew 10x? How did the design evolve?"

This progressive deepening reveals whether the candidate truly understands the system or is just reciting a rehearsed story. A candidate who can explain why they made a decision — including the trade-offs they considered and the alternatives they rejected — demonstrates real engineering maturity.

Technical Q&A: Testing Real Understanding

The technical questions I ask depend on the role, but here are some of my favorites and what they reveal:

  • What improvements did PHP 7 bring? — Tests whether the candidate keeps up with their ecosystem or just uses whatever version was installed when they started.
  • How do array operations work under the hood? — Reveals whether they understand data structures or just treat arrays as magic boxes.
  • What are the PSR standards? — Shows whether they care about community conventions and code consistency.
  • Walk me through what happens when a web request is made. — A classic that tests end-to-end understanding of the stack: DNS, TCP, HTTP, server processing, database queries, response rendering.
  • How does MySQL store data internally? What is the difference between InnoDB and MyISAM? — Tests database fundamentals beyond writing SELECT statements.
  • Is Redis single-threaded? How does it handle concurrency? — Reveals understanding of I/O models, event loops, and why Redis is fast.
  • What are the principles of OOP? How do you apply them in practice? — Encapsulation, inheritance, polymorphism — but more importantly, can they give real examples?
  • Name some design patterns you have used. — Not looking for a textbook recitation. Looking for genuine application: "I used the Strategy pattern here because..."
  • Algorithm questions — Usually something practical: string manipulation, tree traversal, or a problem that maps to a real scenario.

Non-Technical Chat: Books, Blog, Hobbies

I always leave time for non-technical conversation. I ask questions like:

  • "What technical books have you read recently?"
  • "Do you have a blog? What do you write about?"
  • "What do you do for fun outside of coding?"

These questions serve multiple purposes. They reveal whether the candidate is a continuous learner. They show communication skills and personality. And they help me assess whether this is someone I would enjoy working with every day.

A candidate who reads books, writes blog posts, and has interesting hobbies outside of work tends to be more creative, more resilient, and more pleasant to collaborate with.

Candidate Questions: Do Your Homework First

At the end, I always ask: "Do you have any questions for me?" This is not a formality — it is a test.

The worst answer is "No, I don't have any questions." It signals a lack of curiosity or preparation.

Equally bad is asking questions that could have been answered by spending five minutes on the company website: "So, what does your company do?"

Good questions show that the candidate has done their research and is genuinely evaluating whether this role is a fit: "I noticed your team recently migrated to microservices. What has been the biggest challenge in that transition?" or "How does the team balance feature development with technical debt?"

Attitude: Honesty Over Bluffing

Perhaps the most important thing I look for is honesty. When a candidate does not know something, the best response is simply: "I am not familiar with that, but based on what I know about X, I would guess..."

Bluffing is immediately obvious to an experienced interviewer, and it is fatal. It signals not just ignorance, but an inability to acknowledge limitations — which is a far more dangerous trait in an engineer.

The ideal attitude is confidence combined with humility. Confident in what you know, honest about what you do not, and genuinely curious about learning more.

The Evaluation Template

After the interview, I fill out a structured evaluation. Here is the framework I use, with weights reflecting what I believe matters most:

Dimension Weight What I Assess
Technical Skills 35% Depth of knowledge, understanding of fundamentals, problem-solving ability
Attitude 25% Honesty, curiosity, passion for learning, humility
Communication 20% Clarity of expression, ability to explain complex ideas, listening skills
Project Experience 20% Depth of involvement, design thinking, lessons learned

Each dimension is scored from 0 to 10. The weighted sum determines the final score. But scores are not everything — a candidate who scores 7 across the board with glowing qualitative notes is often better than a candidate who scores 9 in one dimension but 4 in another.

Final Thoughts

Interviews are not exams. They are conversations designed to assess fit — fit between the candidate's skills and the role, between the candidate's values and the team culture, between the candidate's career goals and what the company can offer.

As a candidate, understanding the interviewer's perspective does not mean gaming the system. It means recognizing that the person across the table is trying to solve a difficult problem: predicting whether you will be a great addition to the team. Help them solve that problem by being prepared, being honest, and being yourself.
