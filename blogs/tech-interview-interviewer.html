Technical Interviews (Part 1): The Interviewer's Perspective

2020-04-20 | Career

Most articles about technical interviews focus on the candidate's perspective: how to prepare, how to perform, and how to land the offer. Today, however, I want to flip the camera around and look at the process from the other side of the table — the interviewer's perspective. Understanding what goes through an interviewer's mind can make you a far more effective candidate.

Resume Screening: The One-Minute Test

Here is a blunt truth: I spend roughly one minute on each resume. Sometimes less, sometimes a bit more, but rarely much longer. When dealing with dozens or even hundreds of applications, triage is your only option. The goal isn't to find the perfect candidate — it is to identify signals that suggest someone is above average.

Self-Introduction: Not a Grocery List

When a candidate's resume includes a "summary" or "about me" section, I look for one thing above all: does this person demonstrate genuine passion and confidence, or are they just reciting a list of technologies?

A bad introduction reads like a shopping list: "I know Java, Python, MySQL, Redis, Kafka, Docker..." That tells me nothing. Anyone can learn a technology; merely listing it proves nothing.

A great self-introduction reveals character. It highlights what excites the engineer, what they have built, and the problems they care about. Confidence without arrogance, enthusiasm without hand-waving — that is what catches my eye.

Project Experience: Contributions, Not Descriptions

Most resumes describe projects from a product manager's perspective: "This system handles 10,000 QPS and supports features A, B, and C." That is a product description, not a personal contribution.

What I want to see is: what did YOU do? What architectural decisions did you make? What problems did you solve? What trade-offs did you navigate? A line like "I redesigned the caching layer, reducing average response time by 40%" tells me infinitely more than "Responsible for system optimization."

Blogs and Open Source: The Ultimate Bonus

A candidate who maintains a technical blog or contributes to open-source projects immediately stands out. These activities demonstrate several key qualities at once:

  • Passion — they write code and explore concepts even when they aren't being paid to do so.
  • Communication skills — they can explain technical concepts clearly in writing.
  • Consistency — maintaining a blog or contributing regularly requires discipline.
  • Depth — writing about a topic forces you to understand it thoroughly.

If I see a blog link on a resume, I will click it. If the content is thoughtful and well-written, that candidate instantly jumps to the top of my list.

The Written Test: Fundamentals Over Trivia

A good written test shouldn't be a trivia game. I have no interest in asking candidates to memorize obscure syntax or solve brain teasers. I want to evaluate whether they possess solid fundamentals.

A balanced written test should cover three key areas:

  1. Language fundamentals — do they truly understand the internal mechanics of the tools they use every day?
  2. Algorithms and data structures — can they solve problems using logical, algorithmic thinking?
  3. System design and architectural thinking — can they design clean, maintainable, and scalable systems?

One policy I strongly advocate: candidates should be allowed to use Google during the written test. In the real world, engineers look things up constantly. Testing whether someone can find, evaluate, and apply information quickly is far more practical than testing whether they have memorized an API signature.

The Face-to-Face Interview

First Impressions Matter

Whether we like it or not, first impressions shape the entire interview. Punctuality, professional demeanor, and body language are not superficial details; they signal respect for the process and awareness of professional norms. A candidate who arrives ten minutes late, disheveled, and avoiding eye contact starts the conversation on the defensive.

Self-Introduction: Show Value and Ownership

The face-to-face self-introduction serves a different purpose than the one on the resume. Here, I am looking for a narrative. Can the candidate tell a coherent, engaging story about their career? Can they articulate what makes them unique?

The best introductions I have heard follow a simple, impactful pattern: "I am a [role] with [X] years of experience, specializing in [domain]. At [company], I led/designed/built [thing], which achieved [result]. I am particularly passionate about [area], which is what drew me to this opportunity."

Notice the structure: identity, expertise, concrete accomplishment, passion, and motivation. Every sentence adds distinct value.

Project Q&A: Uncovering Design Decisions

This is the core of the technical interview. I pick one or two projects from the resume and drill down. I am not trying to trip the candidate up; I am trying to understand the depth of their involvement and their decision-making process.

I start broad: "Walk me through the architecture of this system." Then I narrow focus: "Why did you choose this database over that alternative?" Then deeper: "What happened when the data volume scaled 10x? How did the design evolve?"

This progressive deepening reveals whether the candidate truly owned the system or is simply reciting a rehearsed script. A candidate who can explain why they made a decision — including the trade-offs they weighed and the alternatives they rejected — demonstrates true engineering maturity.

Technical Q&A: Testing Real Understanding

The technical questions I ask depend on the role, but here are some of my favorites and what they reveal:

  • What improvements did PHP 7 bring? — Tests whether the candidate keeps up with their ecosystem or just uses whatever version happens to be installed.
  • How do array operations work under the hood? — Reveals whether they understand underlying data structures or treat arrays as magic black boxes.
  • What are the PSR standards? — Shows whether they care about community conventions, standards, and code consistency.
  • Walk me through what happens when a web request is made. — A classic that tests end-to-end understanding of the entire stack: DNS, TCP, HTTP, server processing, database queries, and browser rendering.
  • How does MySQL store data internally? What is the difference between InnoDB and MyISAM? — Tests database fundamentals beyond simply writing SELECT statements.
  • Is Redis single-threaded? How does it handle concurrency? — Reveals understanding of I/O models, event loops, and why Redis is fast.
  • What are the principles of OOP? How do you apply them in practice? — Encapsulation, inheritance, polymorphism — but more importantly, can they provide real-world, practical examples?
  • Name some design patterns you have used. — Not looking for a textbook definition. I want to see genuine application: "I used the Strategy pattern here because..."
  • Algorithm questions — Usually something practical: string manipulation, tree traversal, or a problem that maps directly to a real-world scenario.

Non-Technical Chat: Books, Blogs, and Lifelong Learning

I always leave time for non-technical conversation. I ask questions like:

  • "What technical books or blogs have you read recently?"
  • "Do you have a blog? What do you write about?"
  • "What do you do for fun outside of coding?"

These questions serve multiple purposes. They reveal whether the candidate is a continuous learner, showcase communication skills and personality, and help me assess whether this is someone I would look forward to collaborating with every day.

An engineer who reads books, writes blog posts, and has engaging hobbies outside of work tends to be more creative, resilient, and pleasant to work with.

Candidate Questions: Do Your Homework First

At the end, I always ask: "Do you have any questions for me?" This is not a formality — it is a test.

The worst answer is "No, I don't have any questions." It signals a lack of curiosity or preparation.

Equally disappointing is asking questions that could have been answered with a five-minute search on the company website: "So, what does your company actually do?"

Good questions show that the candidate has done their research and is actively evaluating whether this role is a mutual fit: "I noticed your team recently migrated to microservices. What has been the biggest operational challenge in that transition?" or "How does the team balance shipping features with paying down technical debt?"

Attitude: Honesty Over Bluffing

Perhaps the most important trait I look for is honesty. When a candidate doesn't know something, the best response is simply: "I am not familiar with that, but based on what I know about X, I would speculate that..."

Bluffing is immediately obvious to an experienced interviewer, and it is fatal. It signals not just ignorance, but an inability to acknowledge limitations — which is a dangerous trait in any engineer.

The ideal attitude is confidence combined with humility: confident in what you know, transparent about what you do not, and genuinely eager to learn more.

The Evaluation Template

After the interview, I fill out a structured evaluation. Here is the framework I use, with weights reflecting what I believe matters most:

Dimension Weight What I Assess
Technical Skills 35% Depth of knowledge, grasp of fundamentals, and analytical problem-solving ability
Attitude & Culture 25% Honesty, intellectual curiosity, passion for learning, and humility
Communication 20% Clarity of expression, active listening, and the ability to explain complex ideas
Project Experience 20% Depth of involvement, design thinking, and lessons learned from past projects

Each dimension is scored from 0 to 10, and the weighted sum determines the final score. However, numbers don't tell the whole story. A candidate who scores a consistent 7 across the board with glowing qualitative notes is often a much better hire than a candidate who scores a 9 in one area but a 4 in another.

Final Thoughts

Interviews are not exams; they are bilateral conversations designed to assess fit. We are evaluating the alignment between the candidate's skills and the role, their values and the team culture, and their career aspirations and what the company can offer.

As a candidate, understanding the interviewer's perspective isn't about gaming the system. It's about recognizing that the person across the table is trying to solve a difficult problem: predicting whether you will be a great addition to the team. Help them solve that problem by being prepared, honest, and authentic.
