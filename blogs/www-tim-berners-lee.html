The World Wide Web: Tim Berners-Lee's Information Empire

2022-04-14 | History

The Information Explosion

Human civilization is, at its core, a story about information — how we acquire it, store it, and transmit it across generations.

In ancient times, humans gathered knowledge by observing natural phenomena, sharing insights through oral tradition. Then came knot-tying, pictograms, and eventually writing — humanity's earliest static information storage. Writing was revolutionary: it allowed knowledge to transcend time and space. Without it, civilization could advance only as fast as memory allowed.

Beyond speech and localized writing, humans developed long-distance communication — beacon fires, lighthouses, drum signals — each advance in range and reliability paving the way for the telegraph, telephone, and radio.

Information production accelerated relentlessly. During classical Greece and China's Spring-and-Autumn period, humanity experienced its first great waves of organized knowledge. The Library of Alexandria was a monumental effort to centralize this expanding intellectual wealth.

The Renaissance and Enlightenment marked the next milestone. While classical Greece represented the germination of specialized knowledge (philosophy and science still undifferentiated), the Enlightenment formalized intellectual division of labor. Natural philosophy branched into physics, chemistry, biology, economics.

Humanity entered an era of exponential knowledge growth. Today we produce more information in a decade than our ancestors did over a millennium. In ancient China, a scholar needed only master the Four Books and Five Classics to be considered educated. Today, a single introductory physics textbook dwarfs everything those scholars knew. The ancients could aspire to "know everything"; since the twentieth century, no individual can master even a single sub-discipline.

This exponential growth triggered an information management crisis. Confronted with an ocean of data, people no longer knew what to look for or where to find it. Humanity faced the danger of drowning in its own knowledge.

The initial response: build larger libraries. But physical structures are limited, and retrieval became increasingly difficult. Scholars also compiled encyclopedias to curate knowledge. China's Yongle Encyclopedia, Diderot's Encyclopédie, the Encyclopædia Britannica (1768) — all ambitious attempts to map human knowledge. Yet no physical compendium could keep pace with the information explosion.

Vannevar Bush and the Memex (1945)

Computers were not originally conceived as information processors. The term itself is a historical clue: a "computer" was a job description — a human clerk performing repetitive calculations, from census work to astronomical tables. From Babbage's Difference Engine to Hollerith's tabulating machines to Bush's differential analyzer to ENIAC — all designed strictly for numerical computation.

John von Neumann, who would become the architect of the modern computer, famously joined the ENIAC team almost by chance, seeking to solve complex differential equations for nuclear detonation simulations.

After World War II, computers moved from military and academic labs into commercial use. Researchers began exploring broader possibilities. Turing's theory of universal computation held that a computer could compute anything computable, potentially mirroring human cognition — a line of inquiry that became "artificial intelligence."

Another group of researchers took a different path. Rather than replacing human minds with AI, they believed computers should augment human intelligence. They observed that knowledge workers spent only 15% of their time on deep thinking — the rest was consumed by looking up references, drawing diagrams, indexing files. If computers could automate this friction, the human mind would be freed for creative work.

These researchers turned to information management — an endeavor that would propel civilization into the Information Age.

Numerical computation and information processing are fundamentally different. Numerical computing targets mathematical operations — trigonometry, logarithms, calculus. Information processing deals with the unstructured artifacts of human communication: text, images, audio, video. ENIAC's full name — "Electronic Numerical Integrator and Computer" — left no doubt about its creators' intent.

As document volume exploded, physical archiving became unsustainable. Universities needed cavernous libraries; companies dedicated entire floors to filing cabinets. Even with meticulous catalogs, retrieving the right document from a sea of paper was exhausting — especially if you couldn't recall the exact index category.

In 1945, Vannevar Bush published "As We May Think," proposing a machine called the Memex. The Memex was an interactive, personalized digital library. It would store vast collections on high-density microfilm, resolving the physical storage crisis. The user would interact via a desk with dual projection screens, a keyboard, and selection levers.

Crucially, Bush introduced associative indexing, now called hypertext: related documents could be linked, letting readers jump instantly from one reference to another. (Bush pioneered the concept; Ted Nelson coined the term in the 1960s.)

Traditional retrieval systems were centralized, hierarchical, and linear. Looking up a citation in a library book meant physically locating the referenced volume, possibly at another library. Even on early digital systems, a cross-reference required exiting your document and starting a new search. Hypertext shattered this rigid directory model, enabling direct connections between arbitrary pieces of information — readers could follow a trail of ideas and return with ease. This became the foundation of the World Wide Web.

Email and the Advent of ARPANET

In the 1960s, computers were expensive capital assets. Researchers asked: could we network them, letting users at one institution run programs on underutilized hardware elsewhere?

In 1963, ARPA (the US Department of Defense's Advanced Research Projects Agency) launched the ARPANET project — linking heterogeneous computer systems across the country to share resources.

ARPANET adopted an architectural design known as store-and-forward packet switching for data exchange — a paradigm that remains the backbone of the internet today.

Store-and-forward routing solved a fundamental problem. Direct circuits between every pair of computers would scale quadratically — physically and financially impossible. Engineers drew inspiration from the telegraph industry. Nineteenth-century telegraph companies established regional exchanges; stations didn't need direct lines to every other station.

At an exchange, an operator recorded (stored) the incoming message, then forwarded it along the next leg toward its destination. Recording introduced buffering: if the outgoing line was busy, the message waited in a queue.

This is "store and forward." Routing through exchanges eliminated the need for direct connections. Buffering prevented upstream nodes from blocking on downstream congestion. (In packet switching, the switch stores bits until a complete packet arrives, then forwards.)

Packet switching applied this principle to digital data. Instead of a massive, unbroken stream — which would monopolize lines, require huge buffers, and force full retransmission on failure — messages were sliced into small, self-contained packets. Each traveled independently, preventing any single transmission from hogging bandwidth and drastically reducing switch memory requirements.

Packet switching did have overhead: each packet required a header containing routing metadata, increasing the total number of transmitted bits, and the receiving host had to buffer and reassemble the packets into the original message.

Initially only ARPA's computers were connected. Soon university partners — UCLA, Utah, Stanford — joined. Graduate students and researchers began experimenting, forging a collaborative online culture.

By spring 1971, 23 hosts were online. In 1972, ARPANET director Larry Roberts organized a public demonstration at the International Conference on Computer Communications. The demonstration was a watershed moment. Within a year, 45 hosts; four years later, 111.

Small numbers by modern standards, but the network was propelled by a surprise killer app: electronic mail.

Email wasn't in ARPANET's original design. In 1971, Ray Tomlinson at BBN built an experimental message utility — initially a minor side project. Email quickly dominated ARPANET traffic. By 1975, over a thousand active mailboxes. The desire to send and receive mail became the largest catalyst driving institutions to join or build networks.

Recognizing the commercial potential, private providers began building independent networks on ARPANET's open architecture, spawning competing email networks in the late 1970s.

But these networks were isolated silos; users on one couldn't email users on another. To unlock networking's full power, engineers had to solve inter-networking — connecting entirely different networks.

ARPANET's original protocol, NCP (Network Control Protocol), was ill-suited. Designed for homogeneous environments where machines shared similar parameters, a new protocol was needed to bridge heterogeneous systems.

In 1973, Robert Kahn and Vint Cerf began designing TCP/IP. By 1980, TCP/IP for heterogeneous networks was complete. In 1982, ARPANET adopted TCP/IP, replacing NCP.

TCP/IP enabled the interconnection of computer networks worldwide, giving birth to the Internet.

Personal Computers & the Internet

Email drove ARPANET's growth. Commercial companies spawned competing networks, which led to the Internet through the need for inter-networking.

But initial growth was slow. By 1984, only about a thousand hosts — mainly universities, research institutions, some businesses.

What accelerated the Internet was the personal computer revolution.

In the 1980s, PCs swept across the landscape. IBM, Apple, Microsoft competed fiercely. Hardware and software flourished. Personal computers became mainstream consumer electronics and office tools.

Millions of PCs connected to the Internet. People didn't just exchange emails — they began sharing entire documents.

Soon the Internet was flooded with documents organized by no directory structure. Organizing and searching them became urgent.

People developed directory services. Archie crawled the Internet to catalog all downloadable files, letting users search without knowing exact locations. Some tools offered fuzzy filename search.

Unlike today's one-click downloads, downloading then required knowing a file's precise location. The workflow: log into a remote host via FTP, navigate to a directory, download the file. You had to know which host had the document. Directory services solved a genuine pain point.

But these directories organized information like books — in a centralized tree with no relationships between documents. If you read about "Charles Babbage" and wanted more, you had to return to the directory and search again. You couldn't jump directly from one document to another.

Forty years earlier, Bush had proposed hypertext to solve this problem. Could hypertext be combined with the Internet?

Tim Berners-Lee & the World Wide Web

Today, the Internet and hypertext seem a natural pairing. But in the 1980s, they developed independently. Both were hot academic topics; hypertext was already appearing in CD-ROM encyclopedias. Yet no one had applied hypertext to the Internet.

A British software engineer named Tim Berners-Lee changed that. In 1989, he submitted a project proposal to CERN (the European Organization for Nuclear Research) describing what would become the "World Wide Web."

CERN, founded in 1954 by eleven European nations, was an organization with complex personnel structures, numerous research projects, and frequent staff turnover. Organizing and retrieving information — personnel records, project details, technical documents — was a constant struggle.

People were plagued by questions like:

  • Where is this module used?
  • Who wrote this code? Where does it run?
  • Which systems depend on this device?

Critical information was routinely lost when people left — not because they failed to document, but because those who remained couldn't find the documents.

Berners-Lee added: "In the near future, other organizations around the world will face the same issue."

A universal information management solution was needed.

Tree structures were the standard. The Unix file system was one example; CERN's CERNDOC was another. If one document referenced another (like "See also" in man pages), you couldn't open it directly. You had to exit and search the directory from scratch.

Trees didn't express real relationships between information. An article about the Difference Engine might mention Charles Babbage. They are clearly related, yet in a tree directory, they exist as isolated entities. The directory reveals no connection.

Traditional directories managed isolated pieces of information without managing the relationships between them. Hypertext, with cross-referencing, far better reflected how information actually connects — allowing direct jumps between related items.

Hypertext had existed for forty years and was already in practical use. If Berners-Lee's proposal had merely been "build another hypertext system," it wouldn't have been remarkable.

The innovation: his system was not a single system at all, but a globally distributed cluster of systems.

Berners-Lee observed that the world faced information crises: people couldn't find useful information; organizations lost critical data daily; centralized directories were overwhelmed.

Meanwhile, existing hypertext systems were self-contained. System A's links pointed only within System A — never to System B. No single system could encompass all the world's information.

Berners-Lee's vision: what if all the world's systems were one vast information repository, connected by hypertext?

Hypertext transcending system boundaries to form a global information universe — a bold vision.

Not technically impossible. The Internet had already connected computers worldwide. If hypertext could ride on the Internet's infrastructure, it could span all systems.

Berners-Lee called this Internet-based hypertext information management system the World Wide Web.

The Architectural Pillars of the Web

1. URI: Naming and Locating Resources

In a globally distributed system, the first requirement is a universal naming standard. Every piece of information on the Web is a "resource." To ensure any browser can locate any resource, Berners-Lee established the Uniform Resource Identifier (URI) specification.

Today we primarily use URLs (Uniform Resource Locators), a subset of URIs. A complete URL follows this structure:

scheme://user:password@host:port/path?params#frag

In standard web browsing, user credentials and query parameters are handled behind the scenes, so typical URLs appear as:

scheme://host:port/path#frag

This provides a clear roadmap. The scheme specifies the protocol (HTTP, HTTPS). The host identifies the server. The port targets the specific service. The path locates the file or endpoint. The frag (fragment) jumps to a specific section of the document (parsed entirely client-side; never sent to the server).

URLs have a limitation: they are tightly coupled to the resource's physical location. Restructure file paths or change the domain, and the URL breaks — the infamous "404 Not Found."

URLs are also long, cryptic, and historically constrained to ASCII, requiring percent-encoding for non-ASCII characters. As web applications grew complex, URLs became hard to read and type.

To address this, architects defined URNs (Uniform Resource Names). A URN is a permanent, location-independent name for a resource (like an ISBN for a book). While conceptually elegant, URNs require global resolution infrastructure to map names to physical locations, and adoption has been limited. In practice, the web relies on DNS, URL rewrites, and HTTP redirects to manage location changes.

2. MIME: Classifying Content types

Once a browser retrieves a resource, how does it know how to render it? How does it distinguish HTML from JPEG from PDF?

The web adopted MIME (Multipurpose Internet Mail Extensions), a standard originally developed for email attachments.

MIME uses a clean, two-level taxonomy of type/subtype, often accompanied by optional encoding parameters. Common examples include:

  • text/html — structured web pages
  • image/jpeg — compressed digital photographs
  • application/json — structured data exchanges

HTML forms use a composite type: multipart/form-data. The body contains multiple media types — text fields (text/plain) and a profile photo (image/jpeg), separated by a boundary string.

Composite media can even nest other composite media. Thousands of MIME types exist today; the complete list is maintained by IANA.

HTTP: The Empire's Official Language

Organizing the world's computers into a unified hypertext system requires a standard transfer protocol — HTTP. If the Web is an information empire, HTTP is its official language.

HTTP's core function: the client tells the server what operation to perform on a resource. Expressed in the request line:

GET /path/to/resource HTTP/1.1

The request line has three parts: operation, resource, protocol version.

HTTP's genius was abstracting operations into a handful of verbs. HTTP/0.9 had only GET. Later versions added HEAD, PUT, POST, TRACE, OPTIONS, DELETE (verbs are extensible):

  • GET: request a resource;
  • HEAD: like GET, but returns only headers — saves bandwidth when you need only metadata;
  • PUT: upload a document to the server. Standard behavior: replace the entire resource (or create it if it doesn't exist);
  • POST: submit data to the server. Unlike PUT, POST's behavior is defined by the server;
  • DELETE: remove a resource. Semantics are clear, but the server may refuse or merely archive.

The server responds with a status line:

HTTP/1.1 200 OK

Also three parts: protocol version, status code, description. Five classes:

  • 100–199: Informational. The most common is 101 Switching Protocols (used in WebSocket upgrades);
  • 200–299: Success — the server successfully processed the request;
  • 300–399: Redirection — look elsewhere for the resource;
  • 400–499: Client errors — unauthorized, resource not found, etc.;
  • 500–599: Server errors — the infamous 502 Bad Gateway.

The second component of HTTP messages is headers — metadata about the application and resource: media types, content type, body length, caching policies.

HTTP/1.x headers have two key characteristics:

  1. Plain text — human-readable ASCII in key: value format;
  2. Extensible — add new headers freely, extend existing values.

"HTTP is a plain text protocol" means the headers are plain text (HTTP transmits images, video, everything). Compare with binary protocols like IP, TCP, DNS, where headers use fixed-format binary fields.

Plain text is less efficient — Cache-Control: no-cache takes 22 bytes; a binary protocol might need 4 bits. But plain text headers are wonderfully extensible. Binary protocols have fixed-length headers expressing limited information. Plain text can add fields at will — extensibility is a core HTTP design principle.

(This describes HTTP/1.x. HTTP/2 is binary, using frames.)

The third component is the message body. The body can contain any data. HTTP describes it with headers: Content-Type (MIME type), Content-Length (length), Content-Encoding (encoding, e.g., gzip).

Early HTTP lacked Content-Length. Clients detected end-of-body by the server closing the connection — unreliable, since the client couldn't distinguish a normal close from a crash. Content-Length was added and is essential for persistent connections.

Across all three components, extensibility is the core HTTP design principle. This flexibility accommodates diverse resources and gives HTTP excellent backward compatibility (status code 101 exists for protocol version negotiation).

B/S Architecture: Separating Storage from Presentation

A key distinction of the Web: the separation of storage and presentation.

Web resources are distributed across machines running different operating systems, software, and protocols. Presentation had to be independent of storage implementation.

The Web adopted Browser/Server (B/S) architecture. (Here "browser" means any client implementing Web protocols.) The browser handles presentation; the server handles storage. The server only needs to do two things: give the resource a URI and serve it via HTTP. The browser retrieves and displays it.

This seems ordinary today, but it was remarkable. In most systems of the time, presentation depended on storage format — one system's data couldn't display in another. Specific formats required specific applications.

Berners-Lee proposed a "browser" — software that ignores storage and focuses solely on presentation. Call it a "Universal Information Viewer." This application isn't limited to one resource type — it can (in theory) open anything. The ultimate realization of "presentation independent of storage."

In practice, no browser supports every MIME type, so content negotiation occurs. The browser advertises acceptable types via Accept; the server responds with Content-Type. If supported, the browser renders it.

As long as the server returns a supported format, the browser displays it — regardless of internal storage. One browser replaces hundreds of specialized applications.

This architecture made the browser the gateway to the Web, sparking the 1990s browser wars. Netscape and Microsoft were the main combatants. Microsoft, initially behind, prevailed by bundling Internet Explorer with Windows 98. IE dominated for over a decade, producing the infamous IE6.

HTML: Structuring Documents

In the B/S architecture, the browser handles presentation using HTML.

HTML (Hyper Text Markup Language) was designed by Berners-Lee and Daniel Connolly in 1990.

HTML uses tags (a, img, div) to mark up structure, formatting, and references. Browsers organize and present multiple media types within a single document — a hypermedia page.

  • Structure: head, body, div, p, tr, td
  • Formatting: i, b, h1
  • Hyperlinks: a, img, audio, video — embedding text, images, audio, video from servers anywhere in the world

With CSS, HTML focused on document structure while CSS handled layout and styling. Browsers grew increasingly powerful alongside HTML, CSS, and JavaScript — from static pages to elaborate layouts, dynamic interaction, and AJAX for streaming content exchange.

Gateway: Bridging Old and New

When Berners-Lee designed the Web, vast information systems already existed worldwide. Their formats varied wildly; most didn't conform to Web standards. But the Web couldn't abandon this data.

Berners-Lee proposed the Gateway: a format converter that transforms non-standard documents into Web-compatible format before sending them to the client.

In this model, the browser accesses a Web server, which contacts a gateway server. The gateway converts the document and returns it to the Web server, which sends it to the browser. One additional layer — no need to modify original documents or overhaul the server.

Today's gateways serve a similar purpose: protocol conversion. One side communicates with browsers via HTTP; the other communicates with applications via FTP, SMTP, FastCGI, etc. Most modern Web servers double as gateway servers. Take nginx:

Much of the Web's content is dynamically generated. When a browser requests a page, the Web server contacts backend application servers via FastCGI. The application generates the result; the Web server converts it to HTTP and sends it to the browser. The process is transparent — the browser sees only the Web server. The Web server acts as a gateway, converting between FastCGI and HTTP.

With this gateway layer, existing data and services (like email) can be exposed through a unified HTTP interface, enabling rapid integration into the Web.
