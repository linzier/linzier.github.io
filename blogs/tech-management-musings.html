Musings on Technical Management

2022-07-25 | Management

After years on various technical teams — some exceptionally well-run, others disastrously mismanaged — I have accumulated observations on what makes engineering teams succeed or fail. These are not grand theories or academic frameworks. They are practical musings born from raw experience, trial and error, and years of watching others get things wrong (and occasionally right).

I offer them as guideposts for anyone navigating the territory between writing code and leading people.

Proactive Management: People Are Not Machines

The first and most fundamental lesson: people are not machines. You cannot simply assign tasks and expect mechanical execution. People have unique motivations, emotions, off-days, stellar days, career ambitions, personal frustrations, and interpersonal dynamics that no Gantt chart can capture.

A machine requires maintenance; a person requires motivation. Machines need configuration; people need thoughtful organization.

What does motivation look like in practice? It means understanding what drives each individual. For some, it is compensation. For others, mastery of cutting-edge technologies. For some, public recognition; for others, intellectual autonomy. A great manager uncovers what makes each person tick and aligns those personal drivers with the team's objectives.

And organization? That means building light structures and transparent processes that help people collaborate. It means clear roles, explicit expectations, regular communication loops, and healthy mechanisms for resolving inevitable conflicts. This isn't bureaucracy — it is removing friction so talented individuals can do their best work.

A manager who treats their team as interchangeable code-producing units will always be blindsided. A manager who embraces leading people — complex, emotional, motivated individuals — stands a genuine chance of building something extraordinary.

KPIs: The Software Team's Dilemma

Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) work well in predictable environments. In a manufacturing plant, you can measure output per worker, defect rates, machine downtime. On a sales team, you can track closed revenue, conversion rates, pipeline health. Software engineering is a different beast.

Why Software KPIs Are Inherently Flawed

Software development is creative, non-linear problem-solving. It is system design, communication, debugging, mentoring teammates, refactoring invisible architecture that keeps the platform standing. How do you reduce any of that to a quantitative metric?

Count lines of code, you reward verbosity. Count commits, you encourage trivial changes. Count bugs resolved, you incentivize writing buggy code in the first place. Count story points, you trigger story-point inflation.

Every quantitative metric can be gamed — and when tied to compensation or advancement, it will be gamed. This isn't a failure of the engineers; it is a predictable response to reducing creative work to numbers.

Consider OKRs Instead

Objectives and Key Results (OKRs) offer a healthier alternative. Instead of tracking individual output, OKRs focus the team on collective outcomes. The team aligns on ambitious, qualitative objectives for the quarter and defines measurable key results that indicate success.

For an engineering team, OKRs might look like:

  • Objective: Significantly improve system reliability and stability
    • KR: Reduce P1 production incidents from an average of 5 per month to 2 or fewer.
    • KR: Achieve 99.95% service uptime.
    • KR: Reduce Mean Time to Recovery (MTTR) from 45 minutes to 15 minutes.
  • Objective: Accelerate development velocity and delivery pipeline efficiency
    • KR: Reduce production deployment duration from 2 hours to 15 minutes.
    • KR: Increase unit and integration test coverage from 60% to 80%.
    • KR: Reduce CI/CD build and verification time from 30 minutes to 10 minutes.

The beauty of OKRs is their focus on outcomes over output. They incentivize the team to collaborate toward shared goals rather than optimizing isolated metrics. They also leave room for the qualitative evaluations that rigid KPIs lack.

Technical Teams Need Technical Goals

A common mistake is forcing engineering teams to operate solely under business-centric goals: "Launch feature X by date Y" or "Support Z new enterprise clients." While vital, these are insufficient alone.

Engineers need technical goals. They need to feel they are growing as technologists, not churning out features. Goals around paying down technical debt, optimizing performance, modernizing toolchains, or learning new architectural patterns are essential to keeping a team engaged.

If you only measure business features, your team will quickly feel like a feature factory. They will do the work, but the spark will be gone. And uninspired engineers inevitably write uninspired code.

The Pitfalls of Subjective KPIs

To bypass metric-gaming, some organizations pivot to entirely subjective KPIs: managers rate engineers on a 1-to-5 scale. This avoids raw metric-gaming but introduces unconscious bias, favoritism, recency and halo effects, and the fundamental awkwardness of reducing a person's contribution to a single number.

Subjective KPIs also foster an unhealthy power dynamic. When your career hinges on your manager's perception, you are incentivized to "manage up" rather than do great work. Energy redirects toward pleasing your manager instead of solving critical problems.

Recommendation: Frequent, Conversation-Driven Evaluations

If you must evaluate performance, conduct reviews quarterly or semi-annually rather than annually. Annual reviews are too infrequent for real-time course corrections and place excessive weight on a single conversation. Quarterly check-ins are more manageable and timely.

Above all, ensure the evaluation is a conversation, not a verdict. The objective must be helping the engineer grow, not passing judgment.

Democracy vs. Autonomy: Striking the Right Balance

How should technical decisions be made? Collectively by the team, or unilaterally by the leader? This is one of the oldest debates in engineering management, and the answer is: it depends.

A Degree of Decisiveness Is Essential for Momentum

Consensus-driven democracy is too slow for a fast-moving team. If every technical decision — from architecture to coding style to tool selection — requires debate and a majority vote, momentum stalls. Meetings multiply, decisions paralyze, the team grinds to a halt.

Sometimes someone needs to make a call. A leader willing to make a tough decision, cut through analysis paralysis, and say "we are going with this approach" provides an invaluable service. Not every decision warrants a debate.

Excessive Democracy Leads to Paralysis

I have seen teams where the leader refuses to make any decision without absolute consensus. Every discussion ends with "let's think about this more" or "does anyone have reservations?" The result: organizational paralysis. The team drifts, critical decisions are kicked down the road, and the eventual compromise satisfies no one.

This isn't democracy; it is a breakdown of leadership. A leader's core responsibility is to guide, which occasionally requires making decisions not everyone agrees with.

Excessive Autocracy Breeds Disengagement

Conversely, I have seen teams where the leader dictates every decision unilaterally. Engineers become order-takers. Their ideas are ignored, their concerns dismissed. The team becomes rigid, silent, incapable of independent thought.

When the leader insists on being always right, the team stops thinking. They wait for instructions, refuse initiative, decline to challenge flawed ideas. And when the leader makes a mistake — as all leaders do — there is no safety net of engaged peers to catch it.

A Pragmatic Model for Technical Leadership

There is a concept from political theory that maps surprisingly well to technical management: internal democracy, external decisiveness.

In an engineering context, decision-making within team boundaries should be highly collaborative. Members should have a strong voice in architecture, code reviews, process improvements, and task prioritization. They must feel genuine ownership over the technical direction.

When dealing with external pressures — unrealistic deadlines, scope creep, interference from sister teams — the leader must act decisively. The leader shields the team, pushes back when necessary, and makes the executive calls the team cannot make collectively.

This model grants the team autonomy where they have the most expertise (how they build) while providing strong, decisive leadership where they need it most (protecting them from external chaos).

Virtual Teams: The Cross-Functional Challenge

Many critical initiatives require collaboration across multiple engineering disciplines: backend, frontend, mobile, QA, data engineering, and DevOps. These temporary "virtual teams" — which exist for the duration of a project but do not align with the formal organizational chart — present unique challenges.

Cross-Team Initiatives Require Dedicated Ownership

The most common mistake is treating a cross-team project as an ad-hoc initiative that everyone works on "in addition to their day jobs." When engineers face competing priorities between their functional team and a cross-functional project, the cross-functional project always loses. It gets the scraps of time, energy, and focus.

For high-stakes initiatives, form a dedicated project team. Members should be explicitly assigned with clear, agreed-upon allocations. They need defined roles, responsibilities, and dedicated communication loops. Ideally, they sit together physically (or share a dedicated virtual workspace) and run their own focused syncs.

Role Clarity Is Non-Negotiable

In a virtual team, ambiguity is your worst enemy. Who is the overall technical lead? Who signs off on API contracts? Who owns end-to-end testing? Who is the primary point of contact for the Product Manager? If these questions lack clear answers, the project will quickly drown in confusion, duplicate work, and turf wars.

Every virtual team requires a simple, explicit charter: a designated leader, clear boundaries of responsibility, and a defined decision-making matrix. The structure doesn't need to be heavy-handed — it simply needs to be clear so everyone knows how to move forward without stepping on toes.

The Power of High-Bandwidth Communication

Whenever possible, co-locate the project team — same physical pod or virtual "always-on" rooms. The bandwidth of immediate proximity dwarfs any async chat tool or scheduled meeting. Integration questions that might spark a 20-comment Slack thread resolve in a two-minute conversation. Misunderstandings that would otherwise fester die instantly.

Physical co-location isn't always feasible, but the principle holds: maximize communication bandwidth for the project's duration.

The Trap of the "Hero" Leader

There is a common archetype in tech companies: the "hero" leader. The manager who is consistently the busiest, most stressed person in the room. Writing critical code, fixing production bugs, reviewing every pull request, attending every meeting, putting out every fire. Working late nights and weekends, perpetually approaching burnout.

And yet, their team is in a constant state of chaos.

The IC-to-Manager Trap

The hero leader has fallen into a classic trap. They were promoted because they were an outstanding individual contributor (IC) — the most prolific coder, the one who solved the hardest bugs, who shipped features overnight. Naturally, they were rewarded with a leadership role.

But they never transitioned out of their IC mindset. They still write core features, dive into bugs, try to solve every hard technical problem personally. Because they spend all their time on urgent, high-visibility tasks — shipping code, fighting fires — they have zero time for the quiet leadership work that actually makes a team scale.

What the Hero Leader Neglects

Buried in the codebase, the hero leader neglects the strategic responsibilities of their role:

  • Mentorship: Coaching team members, identifying skill gaps, helping engineers advance.
  • Technical Strategy: Thinking about long-term architecture, scalability, and direction.
  • Process Evolution: Refining the team's workflow to systematically eliminate friction.
  • Talent Acquisition: Sourcing, interviewing, and hiring high-caliber engineers.
  • Culture & Psychological Safety: Building an environment of trust and transparent feedback.
  • Stakeholder Management: Building alignment with product, design, and executive teams.

None of these have a red-hot deadline. None produce a satisfying daily git commit. But they determine whether the team will be effective six months, a year, or three years from now. A leader too busy writing code will never build a self-sustaining team.

The Shift in Identity

Stepping out of this trap requires a fundamental identity shift. The leader must stop coding on the critical path. They must delegate the rewarding, visible technical work they love and excel at, and focus on management and coaching — disciplines where they may initially feel less competent.

This doesn't mean they stop being technical. A great engineering manager still guides architectural reviews, facilitates debates, and keeps their knowledge sharp. But they should never be a bottleneck for shipping. They shouldn't be merging hotfixes at midnight. Their deliverable is no longer code — it is the output, health, and growth of their team.

Cultivating the "Spark": Building a Technical Culture

An engineering team without a vibrant technical culture is a group of people who happen to write code for the same payroll. The "spark" — intrinsic enthusiasm for technology, restless curiosity about how things work, shared pride in craftsmanship — is what separates a world-class team from a mediocre one.

A Technical Culture Is the Soul of the Team

This culture is the team's engine of retention and innovation. It makes engineers excited to push their boundaries and take ownership of their craft.

A team with a healthy technical culture is characterized by engineers who:

  • Read technical articles, books, and papers, and share insights with peers.
  • Challenge architectural decisions and propose alternatives.
  • Take personal pride in clean, readable, maintainable code.
  • View code reviews and pair programming as mentoring opportunities rather than gatekeeping.
  • Think about how systems operate under the hood, from database engines to browser rendering pipelines.

A team lacking this culture is defined by developers who:

  • Do the bare minimum to pass QA and close their tickets.
  • Accept flawed designs because "that's just how the legacy system is."
  • Disregard code quality and technical debt because "we don't have time."
  • Work in isolated silos, rarely sharing knowledge.
  • Show zero curiosity about anything outside their immediate tasks.

The contrast is stark, and it is the manager's responsibility to cultivate that spark.

Practical Ways to Ignite the Spark

Culture is built through consistent, deliberate action, not inspirational posters. Several practices are particularly effective:

Regular Tech Showcases. Establish a recurring, low-pressure forum (weekly or bi-weekly) where team members present a technical topic. A new framework, a deep dive into a recent refactor, a post-mortem of a production outage, a book review — the topic matters less than the habit of sharing and learning together.

Reframing Code Reviews. Elevate code reviews from a checklist to a learning channel. Excellent reviews are conversations about design patterns, performance, maintainability. They are the most effective way senior engineers mentor junior colleagues. If your team's reviews consist entirely of "LGTM," you are missing a massive educational opportunity.

Dedicated Engineering Initiatives. Allocate a non-negotiable portion of every sprint to technical improvements: refactoring legacy code, optimizing queries, upgrading tools, automating workflows. If engineers never improve their workspace, they will burn out on the feature treadmill.

The Hierarchy of Culture

There is a natural hierarchy to building a healthy engineering culture. Establish it from the ground up:

  1. Technical Culture comes first. A team that doesn't care about engineering excellence, clean code, and modern toolsets will struggle to care about anything else.
  2. Product & Business Culture comes second. Once the team takes pride in their craft, connect them to the business context. Why are we building this feature? What user pain point are we solving? Engineers who understand the business make better trade-offs.
  3. Service & Operational Culture comes third. Once technically strong and business-savvy, cultivate respect for operational excellence. How do we support users? Monitor services? Respond to incidents? A team with strong service culture takes end-to-end ownership of their software.

You cannot skip steps. A team that hasn't mastered their craft cannot build a great product; a team that doesn't understand the product cannot provide exceptional service.

Conclusion

Technical leadership is a balancing act. You must step away from the keyboard and lean into the messy world of human dynamics, organizational strategy, and cultural development — all while ensuring the product ships and the servers stay online.

There is no silver-bullet framework. Only core principles, keen observations, and hard-won lessons. The guideposts I've shared here may not apply to every team, but they have served me well.

The tenets remain: manage engineers as individuals, not resources; set goals that inspire rather than metrics that merely measure; balance collaboration with decisiveness; build dedicated cross-functional teams for complex initiatives; step away from the codebase to lead strategically; nurture the engineering spark. When you build a team that loves their craft, understands the business, and takes pride in operational excellence, success follows.
