Musings on Technical Management

2022-07-25 | Management

After years of working in various technical teams — some exceptionally well-run, others disastrously mismanaged — I have accumulated a collection of observations on what makes technical teams succeed or fail. These are not grand theories or highly researched academic frameworks. They are practical musings born from raw experience, trial and error, and years of watching others get things wrong (and, occasionally, right).

I offer them here in the hope that they might serve as useful guideposts for anyone navigating the tricky territory between writing code and leading people.

Proactive Management: People Are Not Machines

The first and most fundamental lesson is this: people are not machines. You cannot simply assign tasks and expect them to be executed with mechanical, predictable precision. People have unique motivations, emotions, off-days, stellar days, career ambitions, personal frustrations, and interpersonal dynamics that no Gantt chart can capture.

A machine requires maintenance; a person requires motivation. A collection of machines requires configuration; a group of people requires thoughtful organization.

What does cultivating motivation look like in practice? It means understanding what drives each individual on your team. For some, it is compensation. For others, it is the opportunity to master cutting-edge technologies. For some, it is public recognition; for others, it is intellectual autonomy. A great manager uncovers what makes each person tick and aligns those personal drivers with the team's overarching objectives.

And organization? That means building light structures and transparent processes that help people collaborate effectively. It means establishing clear roles, explicit expectations, regular communication loops, and healthy mechanisms for resolving inevitable conflicts. This isn't about introducing bureaucracy — it is about removing friction so that talented individuals can do their best work.

A manager who treats their team as a collection of interchangeable, code-producing units will always be blindsided when things go wrong. A manager who embraces the reality that they are leading people — complex, emotional, and motivated individuals — stands a genuine chance of building something extraordinary.

KPIs: The Software Team's Dilemma

Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) work remarkably well in predictable environments. In a manufacturing plant, you can measure output per worker, defect rates, and machine downtime. On a sales team, you can track closed revenue, conversion rates, and pipeline health. But software engineering is a fundamentally different beast.

Why Software KPIs Are Inherently Flawed

Software development is creative, non-linear problem-solving. It is system design, communication, debugging, mentoring teammates, and refactoring invisible architecture that keeps the platform standing. How do you reduce any of that to a neat, quantitative metric?

If you count lines of code, you reward verbosity and bloat. If you count commits, you encourage trivial, fragmented changes. If you count bugs resolved, you incentivize developers to write buggy code in the first place. If you count story points completed, you trigger story-point inflation as teams subjectively inflate estimates.

Every quantitative metric in software engineering can be gamed — and when a metric is tied to compensation or career advancement, it will be gamed. This isn't a failure of the engineers; it is a predictable human response to reducing creative, collaborative work to numbers.

Consider OKRs Instead

Objectives and Key Results (OKRs) offer a healthier alternative. Instead of tracking individual output, OKRs focus the organization on collective outcomes. The team aligns on ambitious, qualitative objectives for the quarter and defines measurable key results that indicate whether those objectives were successfully met.

For an engineering team, OKRs might look like:

  • Objective: Significantly improve system reliability and stability
    • KR: Reduce P1 production incidents from an average of 5 per month to 2 or fewer.
    • KR: Achieve 99.95% service uptime.
    • KR: Reduce Mean Time to Recovery (MTTR) from 45 minutes to 15 minutes.
  • Objective: Accelerate development velocity and delivery pipeline efficiency
    • KR: Reduce production deployment duration from 2 hours to 15 minutes.
    • KR: Increase unit and integration test coverage from 60% to 80%.
    • KR: Reduce CI/CD build and verification time from 30 minutes to 10 minutes.

The beauty of OKRs is their focus on outcomes rather than raw output. They incentivize the team to work collaboratively toward shared goals rather than optimizing isolated, individual metrics. They also leave room for the qualitative, nuanced evaluations that rigid KPIs lack.

Technical Teams Need Technical Goals

A common mistake is forcing engineering teams to operate solely under business-centric goals, such as "Launch feature X by date Y" or "Support Z new enterprise clients." While these are vital, they are insufficient on their own.

Engineers need technical goals. They need to feel they are growing as technologists, not simply churning out features. Goals centered around paying down technical debt, optimizing system performance, modernizing toolchains, or learning new architectural patterns are essential to keeping an engineering team engaged, inspired, and motivated.

If you only measure business features, your team will quickly feel like a feature factory. They will do the work, but the spark will be gone. And uninspired engineers inevitably write uninspired code.

The Pitfalls of Subjective KPIs

To bypass the gaming of quantitative metrics, some organizations pivot to entirely subjective KPIs, where managers rate engineers on a scale from 1 to 5. While this avoids raw metric-gaming, it introduces a host of other issues: unconscious bias, favoritism, the recency effect, the halo effect, and the fundamental awkwardness of reducing a person's complex contribution to a single number.

Subjective KPIs also foster an unhealthy power dynamic. When your career trajectory hinges on your manager's subjective perception, you are incentivized to focus on "managing up" rather than doing great work. You redirect your energy toward making your manager happy instead of solving the most critical business and technical problems.

Recommendation: Frequent, Conversation-Driven Evaluations

If you must evaluate performance, I recommend conducting reviews quarterly or semi-annually rather than annually. Annual reviews are far too infrequent to address course-corrections in real time, and they place an immense, stressful weight on a single conversation. Quarterly check-ins are more manageable, timely, and vastly less anxiety-inducing.

Above all, ensure the evaluation is a reciprocal conversation rather than a unilateral verdict. The primary objective must be helping the engineer grow, not passing judgment.

Democracy vs. Autonomy: Striking the Right Balance

How should technical decisions be made? Collectively by the team, or unilaterally by the leader? This is one of the oldest debates in engineering management, and the answer is: it depends.

A Degree of Decisiveness Is Essential for Momentum

Pure, consensus-driven democracy is simply too slow for a fast-moving engineering team. If every single technical decision — from microservices architecture to coding styles and tool selections — requires endless debate and a majority vote, momentum stalls. Meetings multiply, decisions paralyze, and the team grinds to a halt.

Sometimes, someone simply needs to make a call and move forward. A leader who is willing to make a tough, occasionally unpopular decision, cut through analysis paralysis, and say "we are going with this approach" provides an invaluable service. Not every decision warrants a debate, and not every debate yields a better outcome.

Excessive Democracy Leads to Paralysis

I have observed teams where the leader is so terrified of appearing autocratic that they refuse to make any decision without absolute consensus. Every discussion ends with "let's think about this more" or "does anyone have even minor reservations?" The result is organizational paralysis. The team drifts, critical architectural decisions are kicked down the road, and when a compromise is finally reached, it is often a diluted solution that satisfies no one and solves nothing.

This isn't democracy; it is a breakdown of leadership. A leader has a fundamental responsibility to guide, which occasionally requires making decisions that not everyone agrees with.

Excessive Autocracy Breeds Disengagement

Conversely, I have seen teams where the leader dictates every minor decision unilaterally. The engineers are treated as mere order-takers. Their ideas are ignored, and their technical concerns are dismissed. The result is a team that becomes rigid, silent, and incapable of independent thought.

When the leader insists they are always right, the team simply stops thinking. They wait passively for instructions, refuse to take initiative, and decline to challenge obviously flawed ideas. And when the leader eventually makes a mistake — as all leaders do — there is no safety net of engaged peers to catch it.

A Pragmatic Model for Technical Leadership

There is a concept from political theory that maps surprisingly well to technical management: internal democracy, external decisiveness.

In an engineering context, this means that within the team boundaries, decision-making should be highly collaborative. Team members should have a strong voice in architecture, code reviews, process improvements, and task prioritization. They must feel genuine ownership over the technical direction of their products.

However, when dealing with external pressures — such as unrealistic deadlines from upper management, scope creep from product stakeholders, or interference from sister teams — the leader must act decisively and protectively. The leader must shield the team, push back when necessary, and make the difficult executive calls that the team cannot make collectively.

This model grants the team autonomy where they have the most expertise (how they build) while providing strong, decisive leadership where they need it most (protecting them from external chaos).

Virtual Teams: The Cross-Functional Challenge

Many critical initiatives require collaboration across multiple engineering disciplines: backend, frontend, mobile, QA, data engineering, and DevOps. These temporary "virtual teams" — which exist for the duration of a project but do not align with the formal organizational chart — present unique challenges.

Cross-Team Initiatives Require Dedicated Ownership

The most common mistake is treating a cross-team project as an ad-hoc initiative that everyone will work on "in addition to their day jobs." This approach is virtually guaranteed to fail. When engineers face competing priorities between their primary functional team and a cross-functional project, the cross-functional project always loses. It gets the scraps of their time, energy, and focus.

For high-stakes cross-team initiatives, you must form a dedicated project team. Members should be explicitly assigned to the project with clear, agreed-upon allocations. They must have defined roles, responsibilities, and dedicated communication loops. Ideally, they should sit together physically (or share a dedicated virtual workspace) and run their own focused syncs.

Role Clarity Is Non-Negotiable

In a virtual team, ambiguity is your worst enemy. Who is the overall technical lead? Who signs off on API contracts? Who owns end-to-end testing? Who is the primary point of contact for the Product Manager? If these questions lack clear answers, the project will quickly drown in confusion, duplicate work, and turf wars.

Every virtual team requires a simple, explicit charter: a designated leader, clear boundaries of responsibility, and a defined decision-making matrix. The structure doesn't need to be heavy-handed — it simply needs to be clear so everyone knows how to move forward without stepping on toes.

The Power of High-Bandwidth Communication

Whenever possible, co-locate the project team — whether that means sitting in the same physical pod or setting up virtual "always-on" collaboration rooms. The communication bandwidth of immediate proximity is orders of magnitude higher than any asynchronous chat tool or scheduled meeting. Complex integration questions that might spark a 20-comment Slack thread can be resolved in a two-minute conversation. Misunderstandings that would otherwise fester are neutralized instantly.

In our increasingly distributed world, physical co-location isn't always feasible, but the principle remains: maximize the bandwidth of your communication channels for the duration of the project.

The Trap of the "Hero" Leader

There is a common archetype in technology companies: the "hero" leader. This is the manager who is consistently the busiest, most stressed person in the room. They are writing critical code, fixing production bugs, reviewing every pull request, attending every meeting, and putting out every fire. They are working late into the night and through weekends, perpetually on the verge of burnout.

And yet, their team is in a constant state of chaos.

The IC-to-Manager Trap

The hero leader has fallen into a classic trap. They were promoted to management because they were an outstanding individual contributor (IC). They were the most prolific coder on the team, the one who solved the most complex bugs, the person who could ship features overnight. Naturally, they were rewarded with a leadership role.

But they never actually transitioned out of their IC mindset. They still write core features, still dive in to resolve bugs, and still try to personally solve every hard technical problem. They simply try to do all of that on top of their new management responsibilities. Because they spend all their time on urgent, high-visibility tasks — shipping code, fighting fires — they have zero time left for the quiet, important leadership work that actually makes a team scale.

What the Hero Leader Neglects

By remaining buried in the codebase, the hero leader neglects the vital strategic responsibilities of their role:

  • Mentorship: Actively coaching team members, identifying skill gaps, and helping engineers advance their careers.
  • Technical Strategy: Thinking deeply about long-term architecture, scalability, and technical direction.
  • Process Evolution: Analyzing and refining the team's workflow to systematically eliminate operational friction.
  • Talent Acquisition: Investing time to source, interview, and hire high-caliber engineers to strengthen the team.
  • Culture & Psychological Safety: Deliberately building an environment of trust, transparent feedback, and psychological safety.
  • Stakeholder Management: Building alignment and negotiating boundaries with product, design, and executive teams.

None of these responsibilities have an immediate, red-hot deadline. None of them produce a satisfying daily git commit. But they are the exact levers that determine whether the team will be highly effective six months, a year, or three years from now. A leader who is too busy writing code will never have time to build a self-sustaining team.

The Shift in Identity

Stepping out of this trap is incredibly difficult because it requires a fundamental shift in identity. The leader must stop coding on the critical path. They must delegate the rewarding, highly visible technical work that they love — and are excellent at — and focus instead on management and coaching, disciplines where they may initially feel less competent and comfortable.

This doesn't mean they stop being technical. A great engineering manager should still guide architectural reviews, facilitate technical debates, and keep their technical knowledge sharp. But they should never be a bottleneck for shipping code. They shouldn't be the ones merging critical hotfixes at midnight. Their primary deliverable is no longer their own code — it is the output, health, and growth of their team.

Cultivating the "Spark": Building a Technical Culture

An engineering team without a vibrant technical culture is simply a group of individuals who happen to write code for the same payroll. The "spark" — an intrinsic enthusiasm for technology, a restless curiosity about how things work, and a shared pride in engineering craftsmanship — is what separates a world-class team from a mediocre one.

A Technical Culture Is the Soul of the Team

This culture is the team's engine of retention and innovation. It is what makes engineers excited to log in every morning, push their boundaries, and take deep ownership of their craft. It turns feature delivery from a chore into an art form.

A team with a healthy, vibrant technical culture is characterized by engineers who:

  • Eagerly read technical articles, books, and papers, and share those insights with peers.
  • Thoughtfully challenge architectural decisions and propose elegant alternatives.
  • Take deep personal pride in clean, readable, and highly maintainable code.
  • View code reviews and pair programming as valuable mentoring opportunities rather than gatekeeping.
  • Dwell on how systems operate under the hood, from database engines to browser rendering pipelines.

Conversely, a team lacking this culture is defined by developers who:

  • Do the absolute bare minimum required to pass QA and close their tickets.
  • Accept fundamentally flawed designs because "that's just how the legacy system is."
  • Disregard code quality, modularity, and technical debt because "we don't have time for that."
  • Work in highly isolated silos, rarely collaborating or sharing knowledge.
  • Show zero interest or curiosity in anything outside their immediate, narrow tasks.

The contrast between these two environments is night and day, and it is the manager's primary responsibility to cultivate and protect that vital spark.

Practical Ways to Ignite the Spark

Culture is built through consistent, deliberate actions, not inspirational posters. Here are several highly effective practices:

Regular Tech Showcases. Establish a recurring, low-pressure forum (weekly or bi-weekly) where team members present on a technical topic of interest. It could be a new framework they experimented with, a deep dive into an architectural component they recently refactored, a post-mortem of a fascinating production outage, or a review of a technical book. The specific topic matters less than the habit of sharing, discussing, and learning together.

Reframing Code Reviews. Elevate code reviews from a perfunctory checklist to a collaborative learning channel. Excellent code reviews are conversations about design patterns, performance characteristics, and maintainability. They are the single most effective way for senior engineers to organically mentor junior colleagues. If your team's code reviews consist entirely of "LGTM" ("Looks Good To Me"), you are missing a massive educational opportunity.

Dedicated Engineering Initiatives. Allocate a dedicated, non-negotiable portion of every sprint to purely technical improvements: refactoring legacy code, optimizing database queries, upgrading developer tools, or automating manual workflows. If your engineers are never allowed to improve their workspace, they will quickly burn out on the feature treadmill.

The Hierarchy of Culture

I believe there is a natural hierarchy to building a healthy engineering team culture, and you must establish it from the ground up:

  1. Technical Culture comes first. This is the foundation. A team that doesn't care about engineering excellence, clean code, and modern toolsets will struggle to care about anything else.
  2. Product & Business Culture comes second. Once the team takes pride in their craft, connect them deeply to the business context. Why are we building this specific feature? What user pain point are we solving? How does our code drive company growth? Engineers who understand the business make vastly better technical trade-offs.
  3. Service & Operational Culture comes third. Once the team is technically strong and business-savvy, cultivate a deep respect for operational excellence. How do we support our users? How do we monitor our services? How do we respond to production incidents? A team with a strong service culture takes complete, end-to-end ownership of their software.

You cannot skip steps. A team that has not mastered their technical craft cannot build a great product, and a team that doesn't understand the product cannot provide exceptional service. Build the foundation first, then scale.

Conclusion

Technical leadership is exceptionally challenging. It is a balancing act that requires you to step away from the keyboard and lean into the messy, nuanced world of human dynamics, organizational strategy, and cultural development — all while ensuring the product continues to ship and the servers remain online.

There is no single formula or silver-bullet framework. There are only core principles, keen observations, and hard-won lessons. I have shared some of my guideposts here. They may not apply to every team or every organizational structure, but they have served me incredibly well over the years.

At the end of the day, the core tenets remain: manage your engineers as unique individuals, not as resources; establish goals that inspire rather than metrics that merely measure; balance democratic collaboration with decisive leadership; build dedicated, cross-functional teams for complex initiatives; step away from the daily codebase to lead strategically; and aggressively nurture the engineering spark. When you build a team that loves their craft, understands the business, and takes pride in operational excellence, success becomes a natural byproduct.
